Abstract Content teams can review only a few dozen pages a week, so they need to know which pages are about to lose search visibility. Using 30,000 pseudonymized pages from 32 clients in the FlyRank ML Internship dataset, I trained models that use only information available before a 30-day window to rank pages by risk of a >20% drop in impressions. Every page was validated on clients the model never saw. A logistic regression put about 42 real decliners into a top-50 review list (precision@50 = 0.84 vs a 0.54 base rate), while the existing stale-and-visible rule and a "falling pages keep falling" rule did no better than random. The output is a ranked, reason-coded action playbook to order a human review queue. It is decision-support, not a forecast for any single page, and it does not show that refreshing causes recovery.

1Introduction / problem

Imagine a content editor on Monday morning: 30,000 pages across many client sites and roughly 50 review slots this week. Some pages are quietly losing visibility. Others had a bad week and will bounce back. Re-reading everything is impossible, and "refresh old content" doesn't say which pages.

This work supports one decision: which ~50 pages to open first, and what kind of fix to consider (refresh, expand, rewrite the search snippet, consolidate, or leave alone). It turns that into a ranked queue with a plain-language reason for every page.

The FlyRank case. FlyRank produces and manages large AI-assisted content portfolios for many client sites at once, and its own research found that strong pages fade unless they are refreshed. The open question is which pages to refresh this month, across clients whose sites behave very differently. This paper answers it for one real, pseudonymized slice of that portfolio, and tests the answer on clients the model never saw.

Lane: Search Traffic Decline Prediction (closest predefined lane: Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring).

2Data

Source. The FlyRank ML Internship starter dataset (content_refresh_anonymized.csv): 30,000 pseudonymized content items from 32 pseudonymized clients, one trailing 90-day snapshot of Google Search Console and GA4 metrics, plus last-30-day and previous-30-day sub-windows. Most pages are keyword articles (27,207), followed by feedly articles (2,096) and comparison articles (697). Client sizes range from 3 to 7,008 pages.

Inclusion rules. Pages with at least one impression in 90 days and at least 90 days old, de-duplicated by content id. No rows depend on what happened in the outcome window.

Not used. Client and content ids (used only for grouping), the label sources (trend_direction, trend_pct), generator metadata, and every column whose time window overlaps the label.

Public-safe: the data contains no client names, domains, URLs or search queries, and neither does this page. All findings are scoped to this slice, not the full 79M-row warehouse.

3Methodology

Label

A page is declining when its impressions in the last 30 days fell by more than 20% compared with the 30 days before. 54.2% of pages meet that bar. The label measures momentum, not overall page quality.

Features: only what was known before the window

The label compares days 1–30 back with days 31–60 back, so a useful prediction has to be made before days 1–30 happen. Every feature therefore comes from days 31–90 back: previous-30 and first-30 impressions, clicks and sessions (the first-30 window is derived as 90d − last30 − prev30), early momentum between those two windows, content age at the prediction moment, word count, keyword volume/competition/CPC with missing-value flags, content type and intent.

I dropped all 90-day totals and rates (they contain the label window) and "days since last update" (68% of pages were updated inside the label window, so it carries future information).

Baselines and models

Three baselines on the same split: random order, the Week-4 rule (0.5·staleness + 0.3·visibility + 0.2·position opportunity) and a momentum rule ("pages already falling keep falling"). Models: logistic regression and a random forest (300 trees, depth ≤ 12).

Validation and leakage checks

Five-fold cross-validation grouped by client: whole clients are held out, so every number answers "does this work on a client the model has never seen?" The base rate is reported next to every metric.

Leakage checks: (1) adding the label source trend_pct on purpose pushes AUC to 1.00, so the test harness catches leaks; (2) the window-aligned features score about the same as the overlapping ones (0.685 vs 0.668), so the honest feature set costs nothing; (3) ids are never features.

The obvious rules failed on this label. The Week-4 rule finds stale-but-visible pages, a useful editorial signal for a different question. The momentum rule is worse than random: pages that fell in the earlier window tended to bounce back (mean reversion) rather than keep falling.

The simpler model won at the top of the list. Logistic regression beat the forest on precision@50 in all five folds (0.74–0.92 vs 0.36–0.76), with similar AUC.

An AUC near 0.70 is useful for ordering a review queue and weak for judging any single page.

5Limitations & honest framing

  • Observational, one snapshot. Pages updated recently declined less often (51% vs 61%), but editors chose which pages to update. Nothing here shows that refreshing causes recovery.
  • Starter slice. 30k pages from 32 clients, with about 6 held-out clients per fold. A new client could land anywhere in the fold range.
  • Short, noisy label. A 20% swing over 30 days can come from seasonality, tracking changes or a search-engine update.
  • Age as a cohort marker. Pages were published in batches, so content age partly encodes "which batch".
  • Missing data. 26% of pages lack word count and 8% lack keyword data.
  • No live outcome yet. The forward-looking queue has not been checked against the following month.
  • Not a model of Google. These are outcomes inside one portfolio, not a statement about how search ranking works.

6Ranked recommendations (the action playbook)

Each page gets a forward-looking risk score (the model applied one window ahead), transparent reason codes, and one action. Within an action tier, pages are ordered by risk × value at stake (recent clicks and impressions).

  1. Refresh now (~4,100 pages): high risk and still ≥ 300 impressions a month. Update facts and examples, add missing subtopics, and expand pages under 2,000 words. Check impressions, clicks and position after 30 and 60 days.
  2. Rewrite title & meta (~2,200): ranking on pages 1–2 but CTR below its tier median. This is a snippet problem, not a content problem.
  3. Light refresh or monitor (~1,400): risky but little demand. Small fixes only.
  4. Consolidate / prune review (~850): under 10 impressions a month and over a year old. Human decision only.
  5. Protect (~6,700) and monitor (~14,700): leave alone and watch the retrain triggers.

A pattern worth a human look: many top pages earn tens of thousands of impressions but almost no clicks. They are visible but not chosen, which points to intent mismatch or weak snippets as much as freshness.

Never automate: deleting, merging, redirecting or no-indexing pages; publishing AI-drafted rewrites without an editor; changing titles on top-traffic pages without checking the live search result. Every batch starts with a two-minute human check per page.

Retrain / pause when: last month's queue scores below base rate + 0.05 for two months; the decline share moves more than 10 points; traffic features drift (PSI > 0.25); a new client arrives; or a core update or tracking change happens.

7Reproducibility

Everything runs from the public repo with the seed fixed at 42. Open any notebook in Colab or run it locally top to bottom:

  • capstone.ipynb: question, data, method, results, limits and recommendations (mirrors this page)
  • w06_validation_audit.ipynb: split before/after, leakage audit, claim rewrite
  • w07_action_playbook.ipynb: queue, reason codes, human review rules, triggers (regenerates the queue CSV)
  • w05_model.ipynb: first model comparison (Week 5)
  • Metric receipts: work/outputs/capstone_metrics.json, w06_validation_metrics.json, w07_playbook_metrics.json

How AI was used: I worked with an AI assistant (Claude) to draft notebook code and this page. I checked the validation design, leakage tests and every number against the executed notebooks.
